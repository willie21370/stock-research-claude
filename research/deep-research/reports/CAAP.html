Why this advice: CAAP is a high-quality, under-levered portfolio of airport concessions. It is net-debt light, at 0.5x net debt/EBITDA and $861M of liquidity, and has just won its biggest de-risking event in six years. On 28 Sep 2026 the Argentine government gazetted the extension of the AA2000 concession, which covers about 90% of Argentine air traffic, from 2038 to 13 Feb 2049, with an option to 2056. In return AA2000 commits $600M of extra Ezeiza capex at a lower reference IRR of 14.3%. Two things argue against a Buy. First, 2Q26 showed EBITDA −4.5% and margin −4.5pp, and Argentine domestic traffic is now shrinking (−7.4% group domestic in August). Second, the shares have already rallied about 15% in a month. Our house 2026E EPS of $1.60 sits 22% below the 7-analyst consensus of $2.04. That puts the stock on 17x current-year earnings, and the probability-weighted 36-month total return is about +25% (+15.4% price only). That is far below the 100% hurdle, and the stress case is −57%. Existing holders should hold for the longer concession life and the ~3.3% dividend yield. New capital should wait for $22–24 or for proof that the 2Q26 margin dip was one-off.

Executive summary

  1. The Argentine concession has been extended by 11 years. AA2000 and ORSNA signed a contract readjustment on 1 Sep 2026, and it was approved by Decisión Administrativa 27/2026 (Boletín Oficial, 28 Sep 2026). The concession now ends on 13 Feb 2049 instead of 2038, with a possible 7 more years to 2056. This is the largest single value event since 2020. Argentina is roughly half the group's revenue, and the extension adds about 11 years of cash flows to the dominant asset. VERIFIED (press of the official gazette).
  2. The price was more capex at a lower return. AA2000 must prove financing for an extra $600M (VAT included) of Ezeiza investment by 30 Jun 2027 and finish 75% of the works by 31 Dec 2031. The regulatory reference IRR falls to 14.3% from 17%. The return is still above our 11.5% WACC, so the capex is value-accretive, but group capex steps up to ~$330–380M a year in 2027–29.
  3. Operations softened in Q2. 2Q26 revenue ex-IFRIC12 grew 8.2% to $470.7M, but Adj. EBITDA fell 4.5% to $160.3M and margin compressed to 34.1% (from 38.6%). The causes were an Argentine cargo comparison base, lower Argentine domestic seat capacity and one-off costs in Uruguay. August traffic was −0.9%, with domestic −7.4% and Argentina −5.3%. International traffic (+4.5%) and transit (+10.4%) are holding up.
  4. The balance sheet is a strength. Net debt is $381M (0.5x LTM EBITDA), liquidity is $861M, and the board has approved $150M of 2026 dividends. The new commitments can be funded internally. The risk is not solvency but Argentine macro and regulation.

The DCF gives $25.25, and the reverse DCF says the price already discounts ~5.8% revenue CAGR for 10 years at a 41% EBITDA margin. Section 1 — Business architecture, unit economics, moat & contractual visibility

Business model & moat

CAAP is a Luxembourg holding company that runs about 52 airports in Argentina (AA2000: 35 airports, ~90% of national traffic), Uruguay (Carrasco), Armenia (Zvartnots), Italy (Florence, Pisa), Brazil (Brasília and others) and Ecuador (Guayaquil). Revenue comes from aeronautical charges (passenger fees, landing, parking), regulated by local authorities and largely USD-denominated for international passengers, and from commercial activity (duty-free, retail, parking, cargo, fuel, advertising). Moat type: efficient scale plus legal exclusivity, because each airport is a regulated natural monopoly. Competitive insulation: 5/5.

Moat durability: widening over the forecast window. The AA2000 term goes from 12 to 23 years remaining, and Armenia has a reported 35-year extension (ASSUMED; terms not verified). The force most likely to erode it is regulatory, not competitive. Under a future Argentine government (election October 2027), tariff freezes or a renegotiated canon could compress returns. The 2026 reference IRR has already been cut to 14.3% from 17%.

Build-vs-buy / copy risk

  • Incumbent replication: not feasible. A competing international airport for Buenos Aires would need state approval, $2–5B and 10+ years, and the concession exclusivity blocks it.
  • Platform/hyperscaler: not applicable.
  • Customer in-house: airlines cannot self-operate concessioned public airports. The state could re-nationalise, which is a political rather than economic risk.
  • M&A target: the controlling family (~80%) makes a hostile bid impossible. A take-private or sale to infrastructure funds (GIP/BlackRock, Ardian, Vinci Airports, AENA) at a 25–35% premium is conceivable but not signalled. We treat it as a weak floor ASSUMED.

Customer ROI / loss-avoidance

Airlines and passengers have no alternative gateway. Value is not a soft efficiency claim: the airport is a toll on mandatory infrastructure. Pricing power is regulated. International fees are mostly USD-based, while Argentine domestic fees are in ARS and lag inflation. In a budget freeze (airline capacity cuts), volume falls but tariffs do not, as 2Q26 domestic Argentina shows. Customer-ROI defensibility: 7/10.

Scenario worlds

Bear: Argentina's domestic market keeps shrinking (Aerolíneas restructuring, weak real wages), peso tariffs lag a 2027 pre-election devaluation, and Ezeiza capex front-loads. EBITDA margin slips to ~37%, and EPS stalls at $1.45–1.65 through 2028.

Base: Traffic grows 3–4% a year with Argentine domestic recovering from 2027. Commercial revenue per passenger rises 4–5% a year, and Armenia and Italy grow at high single digits. Margins recover to 40–41% once the Q2 one-offs pass.

Bull: Argentine normalisation brings capacity additions from low-cost carriers and a tariff catch-up under the new contract. Armenia extension capex lifts the asset base, and commercial revenue per passenger compounds at 7%+. EBITDA margin reaches ~43%.
