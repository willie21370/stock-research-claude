Section 1 — Business architecture, unit economics, moat & visibility

Model: integrated state-owned tin miner (onshore/offshore alluvial in Bangka-Belitung, Riau Islands; early primary hard-rock), smelter (Mentok) and seller of refined tin ingots, plus tin chemicals/solder and minor non-tin businesses. 97% of H1 2026 tin sales were exported (China 36%, India 12%, Korea 11%).

Moat classification: intangible/regulatory (state-granted mining permits — IUPs — covering the bulk of Indonesia’s tin-bearing areas) plus modest efficient scale. Competitive insulation 2/5: the product is a commodity, and the historic leakage of TINS’ own ore to illegal miners and private smelters (the ~Rp300tn corruption case) shows the moat is only as strong as state enforcement.

Durability: static-to-improving near-term (crackdown, seized smelters), eroding over the window via reserve depletion (309kt reserves → ~10–15 years) and falling alluvial grades; the force most likely to erode it is a policy reversal or enforcement fatigue on illegal mining (watch 2027–28).

Build-vs-buy / copy risk: an incumbent cannot replicate the IUP land bank — permits are state-allocated. A “hyperscaler” feature risk is not applicable. Customers (solder makers) cannot build mines. M&A: TINS is ~65% state-owned via MIND ID/Danantara; a takeover premium is not a realistic floor. A buy-out of minorities by the state is possible but unprecedented — assign no value.

Customer ROI: refined tin is a price-taker input; value is availability of LME-grade metal, not differentiation. Pricing power in a budget freeze: none — LME-linked. Customer-ROI defensibility 3/10.
