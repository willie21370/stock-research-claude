Section 1 — Business architecture, unit economics, moat & contractual visibility

Moat classification

Ero is a Brazil-focused copper producer with a gold by-product. It runs three operations: Caraíba (Bahia; underground Pilar and Vermelhos mines plus a mill, 35–40 kt Cu guided for 2026), Tucumã (Pará, Carajás; open pit commissioned 2024, 32.5–37.5 kt guided) and Xavantina (Mato Grosso; high-grade underground gold, 20,553 oz in Q2-26). The moat is cost advantage plus efficient scale/permits: an existing licensed district, infrastructure and mill capacity that a greenfield entrant would need 10–15 years to replicate. It is not pricing power, because copper is a global commodity. Competitive insulation: 2/5.

Moat durability: roughly static. Caraíba's costs (Q2 C1 $2.76/lb) sit in the upper half of the industry curve and depend on grade sequencing. Tucumã ($2.10/lb) is lower-cost, but its grade declines in later years. The force most likely to erode the moat is BRL appreciation plus Brazilian cost inflation, already cited in Q2 as pushing up C1. If the BRL stays strong through the 2027 budget cycle, Caraíba drifts toward the third quartile by 2028.

Build-vs-buy / copy risk: an incumbent cannot "copy" a mine, but majors can out-compete Ero for capital and growth. Vale Base Metals already holds the Furnas asset that Ero is earning into. A hyperscaler or platform owner is not relevant. Customers (smelters) cannot backward-integrate in Brazil in any useful timeframe. M&A optionality: Ero is a credible target: a sub-$5bn, de-levered, producing copper company with 80+ kt/yr and a Carajás exploration position. Plausible bidders are Vale Base Metals, Lundin Mining, South32, Glencore or a Chinese/Middle-Eastern sovereign-backed buyer. A typical copper-mid-tier premium is 30–40% ASSUMED. This puts a soft floor under the stock in a copper bull market, not in a bust.

Customer ROI / loss avoidance

Ero's product is copper concentrate (plus gold). Its "customer ROI" is simply delivering metal into a structurally tight market where smelters compete for concentrate. Evidence: Ero renegotiated TC/RCs for >$20M of 2026 savings. The value is objectively measured (assays, LME price) and not a soft claim, but Ero has no price premium: it is a price taker. In a customer budget freeze (smelter cutbacks), TC/RCs rise and payables fall. Customer-ROI defensibility: 4/10.

  • Q1-26 consensus conflict: $0.61 (+13% beat) vs $0.52 (+32%) in different sources. The lower beat is used.
  • Model calibration: the EBITDA model reconciles to Q2-26 ($144M) but, run at 2025 conditions, would under-explain FY25 adj EBITDA ($409.7M). The likely causes are corporate costs or realised prices that differ from our assumptions. The conservative and stress cases may therefore understate earnings at low copper prices, which biases those targets down.
  • Net-debt roll-forward: a first draft subtracted full-year 2026 FCF from the 30-Jun-2026 net debt, double-counting H1. It was corrected before publication to start from the 31-Dec-2025 net debt of $501.7M. End-2026E base net debt is $287M.

  • "Gross margin" is a non-GAAP mine cash margin (EBITDA + corporate costs ÷ revenue), not IFRS gross profit, which is net of D&A.

3. Bias check

Upside scenarios (Thesis + Aggressive) carry 30%. Downside scenarios (Stress + Conservative) carry 35%. Base, itself mildly negative, carries 35%. The distribution is skewed to the downside. Our justification: copper is within ~3% of an all-time high, and in past cycles record copper prices have been followed by multi-quarter declines more often than by further gains. If spot-flat copper were treated as the base, the PW 36m return would be about +20%, still far below the hurdle. So the conclusion (no new capital) holds under either weighting.
